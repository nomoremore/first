S2:L1: Language features of news (structure)
S2:L2: The structure of a News Report follows a specific structure. The parts of this structure are identified and defined below:
S2:L3: Headline: The Headline is the title of the article which is made up of 10 words or less. This title has two purposes: to introduce the topic of the article and to capture the readers’ interest, usually through controversial wording.
===== Unit 4 for students.pptx | SLIDE 3 =====
S3:L1: Subheading: The Subheading, which is not always included in an article, expands upon the Headline’s content, if needed.
S3:L2: By-line: The by-line includes the article’s author’s name, and sometimes that person’s occupation.
===== Unit 4 for students.pptx | SLIDE 4 =====
S4:L1: Summary Lead: The Summary Lead consists of
S4:L2: the first paragraph, the 1-2 sentences consisting of less than 30 words that begin an article. The content of this paragraph consists of the 5WH (who, what where, when, why, and how) of the article.
===== Unit 4 for students.pptx | SLIDE 5 =====
S5:L1: Fact-based Paragraphs: The remainder of the article consists of short paragraphs that expand on the 5WH mentioned in the Summary Lead and include quotations from key people involved in or related to the article. The content of these paragraphs is fact-based and organized from most to least important, so that the editor can cut the bottom paragraphs out if needed.

S7:L4: TORONTO - In the cult movie classic Attack of the Killer Tomatoes, mutant  vegetables cut a deadly swath through the community, consuming everyone in their path.
S7:L5: But Canadian astronaut Bob Thirsk says Toronto students cultivating tomato seeds from space shouldn’t worry about like imitating art.
S7:L6: “There should be no fear on the part of teachers or parents about any toxic fruit growing from these tomato plants,” said Thirsk, who flew aboard the shuttle Columbia on a 17-day mission in 1996.
S7:L7: He said he’ll eat tomatoes produced from the high-flying seeds or use them to make salsa or ketchup.
S7:L8: Thirsk visited students at St. Cecillia Catholic School on Annette St. yesterday to check up on their experiments with tomato seeds taken into space by astronaut Mark Garneau.
S7:L9: The Tomatosphere project involves 2,500 secondary school classrooms across Canada growing 400,000 seeds, half of which made the trip to space. Space plants are said to grow faster and taller.
S7:L10: “The space-flown seeds made 170 orbits of the Earth, travelled more than 7 million kilometers and spent 12 days weightless,” Thirsk said.
S7:L11: At the end of June, schools will send results of their experiment to the Canadian Space Agency which will compile the date and make conclusions.
S7:L12: Thirsk said the program is designed to introduce space science to young Canadians. “I can see that in these classrooms we have potential future Canadian scientists and astronauts,” he said. “I find that incredibly exciting.”
