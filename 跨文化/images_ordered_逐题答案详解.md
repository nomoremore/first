## Part III. Case Study Analysis

### 36. Hall's theory: high-context vs. low-context communication

知识点来源：`PPT/Chapter 4.pptx` S53:L3-L9，S54:L3-L14，S56:L3-L14，S58:L3-L11；参考答案 P1:L13-L15。  
案例来源：`images_ordered_OCR.lines.txt` P5:L12-L34。

作答：

Hall's theory explains the clash as a conflict between low-context and high-context communication styles. In low-context communication, most information is in the explicit code and people expect direct, clear verbal messages. In high-context communication, much meaning is carried by context, silence, relationship, status, and indirect signals rather than by explicit words.

Company A, the American side, shows a low-context style. First, the representative pushes a printed contract across the table and wants to go through deliverables, timelines and penalties “point by point” (case P5:L12-L14). This shows an expectation that meaning should be contained in explicit written clauses. Second, the American side asks direct questions such as “Can you confirm acceptance by next Friday?” and “Do you agree? We need a clear answer” (case P5:L17-L25). These examples show direct, unambiguous, verbal communication.

Company B, the Japanese side, shows a high-context style. First, the lead representative nods politely but does not open the document immediately, saying “We will review this carefully” (case P5:L14-L16). The indirect response avoids immediate refusal or confrontation. Second, when pressed on the penalty clause, the Japanese side keeps silent, says the matter may require further discussion, smiles, and shifts attention to the value of a long-term partnership (case P5:L18-L29). Their real meaning is not given as a direct “yes” or “no”; it is conveyed through silence, delay, politeness, relationship language, and the need to consult executives.

Therefore, Company A interprets the Japanese side as avoiding the issue, while Company B feels that instant unilateral commitments could disrupt harmony (case P5:L31-L34). The misunderstanding comes from different expectations about how meaning should be expressed.

### 37. Hofstede's cultural dimensions

知识点来源：`PPT/Chapter 4.pptx` S59:L1-L6，S60:L1-L12，S63:L1-L4，S64:L1-L9，S65:L1-L3，S67:L1-L8，S69:L1-L6，S70:L1-L12，S72:L1-L7，S74:L1-L13；参考答案 P1:L16-L23。  
案例来源：`images_ordered_OCR.lines.txt` P5:L12-L34。

作答：

First, the case reflects individualism versus collectivism. Collectivistic cultures emphasize community, collaboration, shared interests, harmony, tradition, and maintaining face. In the case, the Japanese team remains quiet, exchanges comments internally, avoids unilateral commitment, and later says that instant decisions could disrupt harmony (case P5:L18-L34). This shows that the Japanese side values group consensus and relationship harmony more than an individual's immediate answer. By contrast, individualistic cultures emphasize personal rights, responsibility, voicing one's own opinion, freedom and self-expression. The American team expects the representatives to answer clearly and directly in the meeting, as seen in “Do you agree?” and “We need a clear answer” (case P5:L22-L25).

Second, the case reflects high uncertainty avoidance versus lower uncertainty avoidance. High uncertainty avoidance cultures try to reduce unpredictability and ambiguity through consensus, rules, planning, and established protocols. The Japanese team says “This may require further discussion” and the team leader says they must consult with the executive before proceeding (case P5:L19-L29). This shows caution: they want internal agreement and authority approval before making a commitment. The American side, however, pushes a contract with deliverables, timelines, penalties, and asks for a decision by next Friday (case P5:L12-L18). They try to move the negotiation forward through explicit terms and quick decisions.

Third, the case can also be explained by power distance. High power distance cultures accept hierarchy and authority as normal. The Japanese team leader's comment, “We must consult with executive before proceeding” (case P5:L28-L29), shows that a decision should be approved by higher authority, not made unilaterally by one meeting participant. In lower power distance settings, people are more likely to treat participants as able to discuss and decide issues directly. That is why the American engineer expects a direct answer on Section 5.2 during the meeting.

In short, the Japanese side prioritizes harmony, consensus, hierarchy and careful approval, while the American side prioritizes explicit clauses, direct discussion, individual responsibility and quick decision-making. The conflict is not simply about willingness to cooperate; it is caused by different cultural values shaping negotiation behavior.
